# Methodology — Data, Bias, and What Numbers to Trust

## Primary data: live human voice recordings

The main benchmark tables are built on **20 real voice memos** recorded on an iPhone (10 Russian, 10 English, one speaker — Stas Shupilkin). This is the most unbiased dataset in the repo: it captures real microphone characteristics, natural prosody, actual room acoustics, and genuine speaker habits (filler words, codeswitching, etc.).

Why this matters: STT models are trained to handle real human speech. A benchmark on real voice gives numbers closest to what you'd see shipping the model in production for voice memos, bots, or transcription products.

**Treat live voice numbers as the primary signal.** If you're comparing models for a real application, lead with these.

## Synthetic data: TTS-generated audio

The synthetic benchmark (`samples/manifest_synthetic.json`, results in `synthetic_consolidated.json`) uses TTS-generated audio:

- **Provider**: ElevenLabs (models: `eleven_flash_v2_5`, `eleven_v3`)
- **Voice**: Rachel (multilingual)
- **Audio set**: `elevenlabs_rachel_mixed` — same 20 script texts as live, but spoken by the TTS engine

Synthetic audio was added for two reasons:

1. **Reproducibility.** The live audio files are not committed (they contain one person's voice — PII). Anyone can reproduce the synthetic bench by running `bench/stt_landscape_bench.py` against the committed MP3s without needing to record anything.

2. **Illustrating TTS-induced bias.** TTS audio is cleaner, more consistent, and acoustically simpler than real speech. More importantly, if an STT provider and a TTS provider share training data, model architecture, or audio preprocessing pipelines, the STT model from that provider can look artificially strong on audio generated by the same vendor's TTS — a self-reinforcing effect sometimes called **vendor self-bias**.

### How synthetic changes the numbers

Comparing live vs synthetic results shows this effect in practice:

- Models trained primarily on studio-quality or TTS-like audio tend to score better on synthetic than on live voice.
- Conversely, models robust to natural speech noise, filler words, and prosody variation may rank differently on live vs synthetic.
- **ElevenLabs Scribe evaluated on ElevenLabs-generated audio** is a direct example of potential self-bias: the STT and TTS share the same vendor, so artificially strong performance on synthetic is plausible and expected.

### Noise is a pipeline step, not a property of the file

The `elevenlabs_sarah_noise` set (RU + HY, added Sep 2026) keeps only the **clean TTS masters**
in git. The noisy variants are produced by [`bench/mix_noise.py`](../bench/mix_noise.py) from
those masters: white noise at a declared SNR, optionally a narrowband ring tone, fixed RNG seed.

That split matters for two reasons:

1. **Noise level becomes a parameter you can change without paying for TTS again.** A new level
   is an ffmpeg run, not a new render, so the voice stays identical across levels and the only
   thing that moves between rows is the noise.
2. **It limits TTS bias.** One master feeds every noise level, so the vendor-specific artefacts
   of the TTS voice are held constant while the measured variable (noise) changes. Cross-checking
   the voice itself still needs a second TTS provider — that is a separate exercise.

Each sample carries `noise_level` (`clean` / `moderate` / `heavy`), and results are **never
averaged across noise levels**: `consolidate_results.py` and `build_pages.py` both group by it.
Averaging a clean and a heavy-noise take would erase exactly the effect the set exists to show.

### The vendor self-bias flag

`build_pages.py` marks a row **⚠ vendor** when the STT vendor also generated the audio
(`is_vendor_self_bias`: the model id starts with the sample's `tts_provider`). ElevenLabs Scribe
on ElevenLabs TTS is the case that exists today. Such rows are still computed and published —
hiding them would be its own distortion — but they are not a fair comparison against the other
engines on that set, and nothing in the repo should rank them side by side without the flag.
The flag is derived from data already in `runs_detail`, so runs recorded before it existed get
it too.

### What the synthetic tab on the site is for

The GitHub Pages report ([smolevich.github.io/stt-benchmarks](https://smolevich.github.io/stt-benchmarks)) shows both datasets. The synthetic tab exists to let you:

- See which models handle clean TTS audio well vs which are robust to real noise.
- Observe the ranking shift between live and synthetic — large shifts are a signal of overfitting to TTS-like conditions.
- Reproduce exact numbers without needing personal voice recordings.

**Do not use synthetic results as the final ranking.** They show how models perform on generated audio, not on real speech. The live voice tab is the authoritative benchmark.

## Summary

| Dataset | Bias level | Reproducible | Primary signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Live voice (20 real memos) | Low — real conditions | ❌ (PII, not committed) | ✅ Yes |
| Synthetic (ElevenLabs TTS) | Higher — clean audio + possible vendor self-bias | ✅ MP3s committed | ❌ Secondary only |
| Synthetic + scripted noise (`elevenlabs_sarah_noise`) | Same TTS bias, but noise is a controlled variable | ✅ masters committed, noise regenerated by script | ❌ Secondary; good for *relative* degradation |

Synthetic sets are never pooled with live voice into one score — they live under their own
`audio_set_id`, and every summary in the repo groups by it.
